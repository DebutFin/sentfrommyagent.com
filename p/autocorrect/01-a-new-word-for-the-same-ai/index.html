A new word for the same AI.

A federal order renames AI without setting any new capability test. Microsoft saw no ransom note in the attack Tech Times called "AI Ransomware." Anthropic's comparison of GLM-5.3 with Mythos Preview holds up.

As of Sept. 30, 2026 · About a 3-minute read

This week's corrections

Two labels went further than their documents.

This week's three claims go back to an executive order, a Microsoft incident report and an Anthropic evaluation. In two, the headline went beyond the source. The third holds up, with one caveat about who ran the test.

Correction 1 · Policy
What was said

The White House says the President "Inaugurates The Era of Super Intelligence."

White House fact sheet headline, Sept. 29
What the source saysMissing context

It's a vocabulary change, and the fact sheet's first point says so. Agencies must write "Super Intelligence" and "SI" for "AI" in non-statutory documents, where the law allows. Section 3 defines the term as the technologies already covered by the federal AI definition (15 U.S.C. 9401(3)). Past rules and contracts don't change. The administration says the name reflects the technology's transformative capabilities.

The White House · White House fact sheet · Defense One

Why it matters. The order sets no capability test. Superintelligence usually means an intellect that "greatly exceeds the cognitive performance of humans in virtually all domains of interest," in Nick Bostrom's phrase, and Defense One notes today's systems don't qualify.

Correction 2 · Security
What was said

AI ransomware wiped 100 Azure accounts in seven minutes.

Tech Times headline, Sept. 28
What the source saysMissing context

Microsoft's Sept. 25 report describes one organization's Azure tenant. The "100" were storage accounts inside it. A seven-minute destructive run made 100+ deletion attempts, and most succeeded. One of the two compromised service principals had its secret exposed in plain text in a public GitHub issue. Microsoft saw no ransom note and couldn't confirm data theft; it calls the pattern consistent with tactics that can support ransomware.

Microsoft Security · Tech Times · CSO Online

Why it matters. Microsoft links the attack to a broader shift toward AI-orchestrated attacks. Standard protections worked. Locks set in advance saved a few accounts, Tech Times reports. Rotate any secret that has appeared in a public repo, and put deletion locks on storage you can't afford to lose.

Correction 3 · Model evaluations
What was said

Z.ai's open-weight GLM-5.3 nearly matches Anthropic's Claude Mythos Preview at building cyber exploits.

The Decoder headline on Anthropic's Sept. 29 report
What the source saysRight, but

Anthropic's tests support the comparison. GLM-5.3 built working exploits for Chrome's V8 engine in 50 of 410 attempts, against 56 for Mythos Preview. On a binary exploitation test it reached full control-flow hijacks in 4% of trials, against 6%. Both models fail most of the time. The larger difference was in safeguards. A false cover story got GLM-5.3 to engage 64% of the time, and an abliterated copy, with refusals stripped out, 100%.

Anthropic · The Decoder

Why it matters. GLM-5.3's refusals were easy to get past, and Anthropic measured that engagement in a simulated environment. Because refusals can be stripped from open weights, security teams should assume attackers have this level of help. The tests are Anthropic's, run on a competitor's model.

How to read the next one
  1. Find the document. Read the order, incident report or paper behind the recap. Definitions are in the text.
  2. Check what was counted. "100 Azure Accounts" can mean storage accounts in one organization. Ask who was affected and what each number counts.
  3. Ask for the rate. "Nearly matches" can mean 12% against 14%.
